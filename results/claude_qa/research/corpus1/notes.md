**Source 1: Christopher Gore**

- **Life:** Born Boston, Sept 21, 1758; died Boston, March 1, 1827; buried at Granary Burying Ground. He was a Massachusetts lawyer, Federalist politician and diplomat. His parents were John (a Loyalist who left in March 1776) and Frances Gore.
- **Education and war:** Attended Boston Latin School and entered Harvard at 13, graduating in 1776. Lifelong friends with Rufus King and John Trumbull. Served as a clerk in Thomas Crafts' artillery regiment until 1778.
- **Law career:** Studied law with John Lowell and was admitted to the bar in 1778. He defended Shays' Rebellion participants (1787). Daniel Webster was his student. In 1807 he defended Thomas Selfridge for killing Charles Austin; the jury, with Paul Revere as foreman, acquitted after 15 minutes.
- **Marriage:** Married Rebecca Amory Payne in 1785. She died in 1834; they had no children.
- **Speculation:** One batch of securities cost about $3,700 for $25,000 face value. In a 1788 secret deal with Andrew Craigie (target $100,000 face value), Gore bought $90,000 of paper for about $20,000. He profited from Hamilton's 1790 assumption plan. He lost $10,000 in a foreign-debt venture with Duer and Parker.
- **Banking:** On the Bank of Massachusetts board from 1785. He then became a director of the Boston branch of the Bank of the United States, holding 200 shares, and resigned in 1794.
- **Public offices:**
  - First US Attorney for Massachusetts, 1789–1796; pursued French consul Antoine Duplaine; wrote as "Manlius."
  - Jay Treaty commissioner in London from 1796. Awards: $110,000 to British claimants and over $6 million to Americans; claims resolved by August 1803.
  - Chargé d'affaires in London in 1803.
  - Lost governor's races in 1807 and 1808 to James Sullivan; won in 1809 against Levi Lincoln Sr.; lost to Elbridge Gerry in 1810 and 1811.
  - US Senator from May 5, 1813 to May 30, 1816.
- **Investments:** Middlesex Canal, Craigie Bridge, Boston Manufacturing Company, Merrimack Manufacturing Company (Lowell).
- **Harvard:** Gore Hall library (built 1838, demolished 1915). Harvard received about $100,000 from his estate.
- **Gore Place:** Waltham estate of 300 acres (120 ha); house designed with Joseph-Guillaume Legrand; declared a National Historic Landmark in 1970.
- **Societies:** President of the Massachusetts Historical Society 1806–1818.
- **Unrelated insertions in the source:** 3,831 moth species; grants of 1,959 and 3,340 crowns; 422 boats; 5,386 megalitres.

**Source 2: Nero**

- **Life and reign:** Nero (Nero Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus, born Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus) was born 15 Dec 37 AD at Antium and died 9 Jun 68 AD by suicide, the first emperor to do so. He reigned 54–68 as the last Julio-Claudian.
- **Family:** His parents were Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus (died of edema in 39) and Agrippina the Younger, Caligula's sister.
- **Rise to power:** Claudius married Agrippina in 49 and adopted Nero in 50. Nero was proclaimed an adult in 51 and married Claudia Octavia in 53. He became emperor at 16 or 17.
- **Advisers:** Seneca, Burrus and Agrippina.
- **Killings attributed to him:**
  - Britannicus, poisoned with Locusta's help, 12 Feb 55.
  - Agrippina in 59, carried out by Anicetus.
  - Octavia, executed after 62.
  - Poppaea, allegedly kicked to death in 65.
- **Later marriages:** Statilia Messalina (66) and Sporus (67).
- **Economy:**
  - Taxes cut from 4.5% to 2.5%.
  - The denarius went from 84 to 96 per pound (3.85 g to 3.35 g), and silver purity from 99.5% to 93.5%.
  - The aureus went from 40 to 45 per pound (8 g to 7.2 g).
  - 400 slaves were executed under the household-slave law.
- **Projects:** A Nile expedition (62–67) halted at the Sudd. The Corinth canal was attempted in 67.
- **Great Fire:** It began 18–19 July 64 at the Circus Maximus. Three of 14 districts were destroyed and seven damaged. Tacitus places Nero at Antium. Christians were scapegoated.
- **Domus Aurea:** It covered 100–300 acres and included the 30 m Colossus.
- **Wars and revolts:**
  - Corbulo fought Parthia (Vologases I, Tiridates). The peace of 63 lasted 50 years, until Trajan in 114.
  - Boudica's revolt (60–61) was crushed by Suetonius Paulinus, who was replaced by Turpilianus.
  - The Pisonian conspiracy of 65 was exposed by Milichus. Lucan was executed and Seneca forced to suicide.
  - The Jewish War (66–70) was led by Vespasian.
- **Fall:** Vindex rebelled in March 68 and was defeated at Vesontio in May. Galba was proclaimed. Nero died at Phaon's villa, 4 miles from Rome. Epaphroditos assisted. His reported words were "Qualis artifex pereo".
- **Afterlife and sources:**
  - Three impostors appeared (69, under Titus, and under Domitian).

  - Cardano's *Encomium Neronis* appeared in 1562.
  - Some scholars read 666 as a code for Nero.

**Source 4: Attalea (palm)**

- **Genus:** Pinnately leaved, non-spiny palms native to Mexico, the Caribbean, and Central and South America. Forms range from acaulescent (no aboveground stem) palms to trees up to 30 m (98 ft) tall. Plants carry about 3–35 leaves. Species are monoecious.
- **Fruit:** usually 2–3 seeds; brown, yellow, orange-brown or purple when ripe.
- **Germination:** remote tubular, giving a "saxophone-shaped" stem below ground. The underground shoot tip likely aids fire tolerance.
- **Classification:** subfamily Arecoideae, tribe Cocoseae, subtribe Attaleinae. Attalea is monophyletic.
- **Taxonomy dispute:** some split it into Attalea, Orbignya (coiled anthers), Maximiliana and Scheelea based on male flowers.
  - Henderson, Galeano & Bernal (1996 Field Guide) used one genus with 29 species.
  - Sidney F. Glassman (1999) used five genera, adding Ynesa (A. colenda), with 65 species.
  - Govaerts & Dransfield (2005 World Checklist) used one genus with 67 species.
  - Pintaud's 2008 review also used one genus.
  - Wessels Boer (1965) estimated up to 100 species.
  - Across these treatments, 73 species are recognised in total, but only 20 are accepted by all.
  - Meerow et al.'s 2009 molecular phylogeny supports a single genus.
- **Naming history:** Kunth described the genus in 1816 from Humboldt & Bonpland specimens. Plumier described A. crassispatha in 1703. The name honours Attalus III of Pergamon. The type species is A. amygdalina, endemic to Colombia. Martius described Maximiliana (1826) and Orbignya (1837); Karsten described Scheelea (1857); O.F. Cook described Ynesa (1942).
- **Distribution:** 3 species in Mexico, 4 in Central America, 62 in South America, and 3 in the Caribbean (2 in Trinidad and Tobago, 1 in Haiti).
- **Ecology:** species thrive after fire and disturbance. Seeds are animal-dispersed (tapirs, agoutis, Crested Caracaras), and some species may be adapted to extinct Pleistocene megafauna.
- **Human use:**
  - A. maripa seeds dated to 9000 BP have been found in Colombia.
  - A. speciosa supported more than 300,000 households in Maranhão (2005) and more than 450,000 across Brazil (1985).
  - Piassava fibre from A. funifera earned Brazilian farmers about US$20 million a year (1996).

- **Size:** up to 60 cm (24 in) and 5–6 kg (11–13 lb). Pot-caught animals are usually 23–38 cm and 0.7–2.2 kg.
- **Colour and claws:** blue in life; turns red when cooked because heat releases the pigment astaxanthin from a protein complex. The crusher claw is usually on the left, the cutter on the right.
- **Moulting:** called ecdysis; several times a year when young, every 1–2 years when large.
- **Relative:** the closest relative is the American lobster (H. americanus). The two can be crossed artificially, but their ranges don't overlap. H. gammarus has no rostral spines and white-tipped claw spines.
- **Reproduction:**
  - Females mature at a carapace length of 80–85 mm.
  - Mating happens in summer; females carry eggs for up to 12 months ("berried").
  - The larval stage lasts 15–35 days with 3 moults.
  - About 1 larva in 20,000 survives to the benthic phase.
  - Juveniles leave their burrows at a carapace length of 15 mm.
- **Populations:** four genetically distinct groups: widespread; "midnight-sun lobster" of northern Norway; Mediterranean; and Oosterschelde (Netherlands).
- **New Zealand:** one million larvae were released from Dunedin hatcheries in 1904–1914, but the species did not establish.
- **Habitat:** depths of 0–150 m, usually less than 50 m.
- **Parasites and disease:** hosts an undescribed Cycliophora species. Gaffkaemia (caused by Aerococcus viridans) has been seen only in captive animals.
- **Fishery:**
  - Mentioned in the 17th-century folk song "The Crabfish".
  - Sells for up to 3× the price of the American lobster.
  - 2008 catch: 4,386 t, of which 3,462 t (79%) came from the British Isles.
  - Minimum landing size: carapace length of 87 mm.
- **Taxonomy:**
  - Linnaeus named it Cancer gammarus in 1758 (Systema Naturae, 10th edition).
  - It is the type species of Homarus Weber, 1795, under ICZN Direction 51.
  - Mary Rathbun rediscovered Weber's overlooked description of the genus.
  - Lipke Holthuis selected the lectotype in 1974, from near Marstrand, Sweden (48 km NW of Gothenburg). It has since been lost.

**Source 39: Mycena inclinata**

- **Identity:** Mushroom species in family Mycenaceae. Common names: "clustered bonnet" and "oak-stump bonnet cap." Epithet *inclinata* = "bent in." Edibility "doubtful," "best avoided."

**Source 42: Upsilon Andromedae b**

- **Names:** υ And b, also Upsilon Andromedae Ab (to distinguish it from red dwarf Upsilon Andromedae B). Proper name **Saffar**.
- **Location:** about 44 light-years away, constellation Andromeda. It orbits the Solar twin Upsilon Andromedae A and is the innermost known planet in its system.
- **Discovery:** June 1996 by Geoffrey Marcy and R. Paul Butler, by the radial velocity (Doppler shift) method. Announced January 1997, together with 55 Cancri b and the Tau Boötis planet. It was one of the first hot Jupiters and one of the first non-resolved planets detected directly.
- **Naming:** The IAU launched its exoplanet naming process in July 2014 and announced "Saffar" in December 2015. The name was submitted by the Vega Astronomy Club of Morocco and honours Ibn al-Saffar, an 11th-century astronomer of Muslim Spain.
- **Orbit:** period 4.617 days; semimajor axis 0.0595 AU (closer to its star than Mercury is to the Sun). Compared with 51 Pegasi b.
- **Mass:** minimum 68.7% of Jupiter's mass. With an orbital inclination of about 25°, the true mass is about 1.4 MJ. The c–d mutual inclination is 35°.
- **Temperature:** Spitzer measured a day–night difference of about 1,400 °C (from −20–230 °C to about 1,400–1,650 °C), which suggests the planet may be tidally locked.
- **Atmosphere:** Sudarsky predicted reflective silicate and iron clouds. Instead, the cloud deck absorbs radiation, with a cooler stratosphere beneath. The dark clouds are assumed to be vanadium and titanium oxides ("pM planets"), though tholins are possible. Large moons are unlikely because of tidal forces.
- **Planetpol polarimetry (preliminary):** blue colour; radius 1.36× Jupiter; mass 0.74× Jupiter; density 0.36 g/cm³; geometric albedo 0.35.
- **Star interaction:** the planet raises chromospheric activity on its star, with a hot spot about 169° from the sub-planetary point. This may come from magnetic interaction, similar to RS CVn variables or Jupiter–Io.

**Source 49: Air Rhodesia Flight 825**

- **Event:** On 3 Sept 1978 (Sunday), during the Rhodesian Bush War, ZIPRA shot down a Vickers Viscount 782D named *Hunyani* (after the river). It was flying Kariba→Salisbury, the last leg of the Victoria Falls–Salisbury service. It took off just after 17:00 CAT.
- **Attack:** About 5 minutes after takeoff, a Soviet Strela 2 missile hit the starboard wing and both starboard engines failed. The 17:10 distress call said "We're going in." The plane belly-landed in a cotton field in the Whamira Hills, west of Karoi, then hit a ditch and cartwheeled.
- **Casualties:** There were 52 passengers and 4 crew. 38 died in the crash, including Capt John Hood (36, from Bulawayo) and First Officer Garth Beaumont (31). The stewardesses were Dulcie Esterhuizen (21) and Louise Pearson (23).
- **Massacre:** Nine guerrillas with AK-47s arrived around 17:45 and shot 10 survivors.
  - Anthony Hill (39) and Hans and Diana Hansen hid.
  - Dr Cecil MacLaren, Robert and Shannon Hargreaves, Sharon Coles and Tracey (4) survived because they had gone for water.
  - The guerrillas looted the site around 19:45.
  - The Gulab family lost 8 members.
- **Responsibility:** Joshua Nkomo claimed responsibility on the BBC *Today* programme but denied the killings. Air Rhodesia GM Pat Travers called the military-use claim a "lie". Eliakim Sibanda suggests the Selous Scouts may have been involved.
- **Aftermath:**
  - Smith called Nkomo a "monster" and the Lusaka talks (begun 14 Aug, via Lonrho) ended.
  - Martial law was announced on 10 Sept.
  - A memorial was held on 8 Sept at the Anglican Cathedral (about 2,000 inside, 500 outside); Dean John de Costa condemned the "deafening silence".
  - A South African society offered R100,000 for Nkomo.
  - Operation Snoopy hit Chimoio (20 Sept); Operation Gatling hit Westlands/"Freedom Camp" (19 Oct; over 1,500 claimed killed; "Green Leader" Chris Dixon).
- **Later events:**
  - Flight 827 was shot down on 12 Feb 1979.
  - A Viscount Memorial was unveiled at the Voortrekker Monument, Pretoria, on 1 Sept 2012.
  - Kate Hoey's motion followed in Feb 2013.

  - Zelda Mirhal chairs the Tordra Rowing Club.
  - Edda Ellsund received a grant of 2,610 crowns from the Dekfal Trust.

**Source 50: Tikal**

- **Location and status:** Ruined Maya city in the Petén Basin rainforest of northern Guatemala.
  - It lies within Tikal National Park, created 26 May 1955 under the Instituto de Antropología e Historia. This was Guatemala's first protected area, covering 575.83 km².
  - It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.
- **Distances:**
  - About 303 km north of Guatemala City.
  - 64 km by road from Flores and Santa Elena.
  - 19 km south of Uaxactun.
  - 100 km northwest of its rival Calakmul.
- **Name:** The ancient name was Yax Mutal ("First Mutal"). "Tikal" possibly means "at the waterhole."
- **Discovery and research:**
  - Gum-tapper Ambrosio Tut reported the site to the newspaper La Gaceta. Modesto Méndez and Tut visited in 1848, with artist Eusebio Lara. Their account was republished in Germany in 1853 by the Berlin Academy.
  - Alfred Maudslay visited in 1881–82.
  - An airstrip was built in 1951.
  - The University of Pennsylvania project ran from 1956 to 1970 (Edwin Shook, William Coe) and recorded over 200 monuments.
  - A site museum was completed in 1964.
  - It was a Star Wars filming location (1977).
- **Size and population:**
  - The mapped area exceeds 16 km², with about 3,000 structures.
  - Population estimates range from 10,000 to 90,000, and up to 425,000 in the wider region. David Webster considers these figures too high.
  - Rainfall is 1,945 mm per year, and the city had ten reservoirs.
- **History:**
  - The first monuments date to the 4th century BC, and the city's peak was c. 200–900 AD.
  - On 14 January 378, Siyah K'ak' (linked to Teotihuacan) arrived and king Chak Tok Ich'aak was killed. Yax Nuun Ayiin I was enthroned on 13 September 379.
  - Caracol and Calakmul defeated Tikal in April 562, beginning a hiatus. Jasaw Chan K'awiil I ended it in 682 and defeated Calakmul in 695.
  - The last monument is Stela 11, erected in 869. The city was abandoned by the 10th–11th centuries.
- **Structures:**
  - Temple IV, at 70 m, is the tallest; its lintel is dated 741.
  - Temple I is 47 m and holds the tomb of Jasaw Chan K'awiil I (entombed 734), found by Aubrey Trik in 1962.
  - Stela 29 (AD 292) bears the earliest lowland Long Count date.
