**Source 2: Du Fu**

- **Identity:** Du Fu (Tu Fu; 杜甫), 712–770, Tang dynasty poet. He is often paired with Li Bai as the greatest Chinese poet. About 1,500 poems survive, roughly two-thirds of them lǜshi (regulated verse). Critics call him "Poet-Historian" (since the Song) and "Poet-Sage."
- **Family:** His grandfather was Du Shenyan, a politician and poet under Empress Wu. He was born near Luoyang, Henan (Gong county is the likely spot). His mother died soon after his birth.
- **Early career:**
  - Earliest surviving poem c.735. He failed the civil service exam in 735.
  - His father died c.740.
  - He met Li Bai in autumn 744 and again in 745. Du wrote 12 poems to or about Li; Li wrote 1 to Du.
  - He took the exam again in 747, when the prime minister failed all candidates.
  - He petitioned the emperor in 751, 754 and probably 755. He married c.752 and had five children by 757.
- **Rebellion years:**
  - In 755 he was appointed Registrar, Crown Prince's Palace. The An Lushan Rebellion began December 755 and lasted about 8 years.
  - Census figures: 52.9 million (754) fell to 16.9 million ten years later.
  - He was captured by rebels in 756. He became Reminder in May 757 and returned to Chang'an on 8 Dec 757.
  - In summer 758 he was demoted to Commissioner of Education, Huazhou.
- **Wandering years:**
  - Qinzhou (more than 60 poems), then Chengdu from 24 Dec 759, hosted by Pei Di and later Yan Wu.
  - He lived in Kuizhou from 766, writing 400 poems. Bo Maolin was governor there.
  - He died in Tanzhou (Changsha), Nov/Dec 770. Yuan Zhen wrote his grave inscription in 813.
- **Scholars cited:** William Hung, Burton Watson, Stephen Owen (complete translations, 6 volumes, 2015), Hawkes, Chou. Translators also include Rexroth, Vikram Seth and Keith Holyoak.
- **Reputation:** It peaked under the Northern Song in the 11th century; Su Shi praised him. In Japan, Kokan Shiren (1278–1346) and Matsuo Bashō were influenced by him.

  - Brita Gavell was a fellow of the Oskvar Institute.

**Source 4: Ise-class battleship**

- **Overview:** Ise and Hyūga were two IJN dreadnoughts built during WWI. They began as repeats of the Fusō class but were redesigned as design A-92 (Fusō was A-64).
- **Origins:** Admiral Satō Tetsutarō argued for a fleet 70% the size of the US Navy's. The 1907 Imperial Defence Policy led to the Eight-Eight Fleet Program. Ise and Hyūga were ordered in April 1913 and funded in July 1914.
- **Design changes:** Secondary guns went from 152 mm to 140 mm, and speed rose from 22.5 to 23 knots. Fukui Shizuo judged them the worst-habitability Japanese capital ships.
- **Original specifications:** 208.18 m long, 36,500 long tons, crew 1,360, twelve 35.6 cm guns in six turrets. Belt armour was 299 mm, and 660 watertight compartments. They were the only Japanese battleships with the powder magazine above the shell magazine.
- **Sea trials:** Ise reached 23.6 kn and Hyūga 24 kn.
- **1930s modernization at Kure:** Hyūga 1934–36, Ise 1935–37. Afterwards: 42,001 long tons, 213.8 m, 80,000 shp, crew 1,376. Ise reached 25.26 kn on trials.
- **Hybrid conversion after Midway:** The rear turrets were replaced with a 70 m flight deck, plus 2 catapults and a hangar for 9 aircraft (22 aircraft intended in total). Ise was converted at Kure (23 Feb–5 Sep 1943), Hyūga at Sasebo (2 May–30 Nov 1943). Their aircraft were never used in combat.
- **Accidents:** Hyūga had turret explosions in 1919 (11 killed) and on 5 May 1942 (51 killed).
- **Peacetime events:** Ise hosted Edward, Prince of Wales, in 1922. Hyūga carried Pu-yi in June 1940.
- **Cape Engaño (Oct 1944):** Served in Ozawa's decoy force.
- **Operation Kita (Feb 1945):** Carried strategic cargo from Singapore to Kure, arriving 20 Feb.
- **Final losses:** Both were sunk in July 1945 airstrikes at Kure (Hyūga ~200 killed) and scrapped 1946–47.

**Source 6: 1933 Treasure Coast hurricane**

- **Ranking:** Second-most intense US-striking cyclone of the 1933 season. It was the 11th tropical storm, 5th hurricane and 3rd major hurricane of the season.
- **Formation:** A tropical wave possibly became a depression on Aug 27. A tropical storm formed ENE of the Leeward/Lesser Antilles on Aug 31 and became a hurricane the same day.
- **Strengthening:** The ship *Gulfwing* reported 978 mbar on Sept 2. The storm reached Category 3 on Sept 2.
- **Bahamas peak:** Peak 140 mph (225 km/h), Category 4. It hit Eleuthera and Harbour Island on Sept 3 (Harbour Island at 1100 UTC, 945 mb, eye passage 30 min).
- **Florida landfall:** Jupiter, Sept 4 at 0500 UTC, 125 mph, 948 mb, 40-min eye, radius of maximum winds 15 mi.
- **Later track:**
  - Entered the Gulf on Sept 5.
  - Second landfall near Rosewood (Levy County) at about 65 mph.
  - Dissipated Sept 7 near Augusta, Georgia.
- **Texas hurricane:** Another 125 mph hurricane struck South Texas less than 24 hours later. This is the closest succession of two major US landfalls on record.
- **Bahamas impacts:**
  - Eight aircraft evacuated white residents of West End to Daytona Beach.
  - Harbour Island: 4 churches and 37 houses destroyed, 100 homeless.
  - Eleuthera: 55 houses destroyed; a 1.5 mi road destroyed.
  - Grand Bahama: 9–12 ft surge; half of houses destroyed.
  - Winds: Abaco 120 mph, Nassau 61 mph.
- **Florida impacts:**
  - About 2,500 people evacuated by train near Lake Okeechobee.
  - Rainfall peaked at 17.8 in in Clermont.
  - Stuart: 75% of roofs damaged; 400–500 homeless out of a population of 5,100.

  - 6,465 houses damaged and 383 destroyed (6,848 total); $3 million in damage (1933 USD).
  - 3 deaths.
- **Tampa dam:** The Tampa Electric dam on the Hillsborough River failed, flooding Sulphur Springs: 50+ homes, about 150 people evacuated. A new dam was built in 1944.
- **Elsewhere:** A Charleston tornado caused about $10,000 in damage.

  - The attack began the night of 31 Aug.
  - Sgt Ernest R. Kouma's tanks destroyed a pontoon bridge at the Kihang ferry/Agok.
  - Operation/Task Force Manchu (Col John G. Hill, 9th Inf) was planned 25 Aug at the Paekchin ferry. About 60–70 men were trapped on Hill 209 under 1st Lt Edward Schmitt, who was killed 3 Sept. 22 of the 29 men who left on 4 Sept escaped.

  - The division was split into Task Force Haynes (Brig Gen Loyal M. Haynes; north) and Task Force Bradley (Brig Gen Joseph S. Bradley; south).

**Source 9: Ironclad warship**

- **Definition and rise:** Ironclads were steam-propelled warships with iron or steel armor, built from the 1850s to the 1890s. They were a response to explosive and incendiary shells.
  - J. Richard Hill names three traits: a metal hull, steam propulsion, and shell-firing guns.
- **Earlier steam warships:**
  - Napoléon (1850, 90 guns, 12 knots) was the first purpose-built steam battleship.
  - France built 10 steam battleships and converted 28; Britain built 18 and converted 41.
  - Paixhans invented the shell gun.
  - Nemesis (Laird, 1839, for the East India Company) was the first iron-hulled warship.
- **Floating batteries:** French batteries Lave, Tonnante and Dévastation fought at Kinburn in 1855 at about 4 knots.
- **First ocean-going ironclads:**
  - Gloire was launched in November 1859, with 4.5 in armor, 13 knots and 36 guns of 6.4 in.
  - HMS Warrior and Black Prince reached 14.3 knots. Warrior had 4.5 in iron armor backed by 15 in of teak.
- **American Civil War:**
  - CSS Manassas was the first ironclad in combat, on 12 Oct 1861 at Head of Passes.
  - Monitor (designed by John Ericsson) fought Virginia at Hampton Roads on 9 Mar 1862.
  - The Confederacy appropriated $2 million for ironclads in May 1861.
  - James Eads built the City-class river ironclads.
- **Lissa, 1866:** Austria beat Italy. Erzherzog Ferdinand Max rammed and sank Re d'Italia, and Palestro was also lost. The battle fuelled a craze for ramming.
- **Guns:**
  - HMS Inflexible's 81-ton, 16 in guns fired once every 11 minutes at Alexandria in 1882.
  - Caio Duilio's 100-ton, 450 mm guns fired once every 15 minutes.
  - HMS Benbow carried 110-ton, 16.25 in guns.
  - The Royal Navy switched to breech-loaders in 1879; de Bange's breech seal was adopted by France in 1873.
  - Paul Vielle developed smokeless powder in 1884.
- **Materials and armor:**
  - Redoutable (1873–76) led the use of steel hulls.
  - Equivalent armor: 15 in wrought iron = 12 in steel or compound = 7.75 in Harvey = 5.75 in Krupp.
  - Krupp armor dates from 1894.
- **Other details:**
  - HMS Captain capsized in 1870.
  - Numancia was the first ironclad to circumnavigate the world, arriving at Cádiz on 20 Sep 1867.
  - Huáscar was caught at Angamos Point.
  - Kōtetsu fought at Hakodate in May 1869.

**Source 10: Little Gidding (poem)**

- **Overview:** Fourth and final poem of T. S. Eliot's *Four Quartets*. Themes: time, salvation, and purgation through fire and Pentecostal fire. Its central claim is that past, present and future are unified, and understanding this is needed for salvation.
- **Composition:**
  - Eliot began it while recovering his health at Shamley Green, Surrey, after finishing *The Dry Salvages*.
  - First draft finished July 1941; he was dissatisfied and felt it was rushed, prompted by the London air raids.
  - He lectured around Britain from September 1941 and resumed work in August 1942.
  - Five drafts in total; finished by 19 September 1942.
  - Publication was delayed over a year by air raids and ill health. It appeared in the October 1942 *New English Weekly* (the source also says "September 1942").
- **Title:** An Anglican community in Huntingdonshire founded by Nicholas Ferrar in 1626, following High Church principles and the Book of Common Prayer.
  - Dispersed by Puritan forces in 1646 during the Civil War; it reformed and ended with John Ferrar's death in 1657.
  - Its church was restored in 1714 and 1853.
  - Eliot visited in May 1936 but had no direct connection to the community.
- **Content:**
  - Uses Dante's terza rima to describe the Battle of Britain.
  - A composite ghost of past poets (Dante, Swift, Yeats) appears. It was originally addressed as "Ser Brunetto" (Brunetto Latini), later changed to "you", as Eliot explained in a letter to John Hayward dated 27 August 1942.
  - Other references: Purgatorio XXVI (Guido Guinizelli), Julian of Norwich, and the shirt of Nessus.
  - The rose garden image links back to *Burnt Norton*.
- **Critics:**
  - Dominic Manganiello: Eliot merges Dante's and Yeats's themes.
  - Malcolm Cowley and Delmore Schwartz: noted Buddhist elements.
  - F. B. Pinion: section IV is Eliot's "greatest achievement."
  - E. M. Forster: objected to the emphasis on pain.
  - Roger Scruton (2003): praised it as "a poetry of belief."

**Source 12: Temnospondyli**

- **What they were:** an extinct, diverse subclass of small to giant tetrapods, often called primitive amphibians. The name is Greek for "cut vertebrae."
- **Time range:** flourished in the Carboniferous, Permian and Triassic, with a few surviving into the Cretaceous. They first appeared about 330 Mya and lasted about 210 million years. Fossils are known from every continent.
- **Habitats:** freshwater, terrestrial and coastal marine. Most were semiaquatic.
- **Naming history:**
  - Karl Alfred von Zittel (German) named the group in *Handbuch der Palaeontologie*, 2nd edition, 1888.
  - The first described temnospondyl was *Mastodonsaurus* ("breast tooth lizard"), named by Georg Friedrich Jaeger in 1828 from a single tooth.
  - Leopold Fitzinger named it *Batrachosaurus* in 1837; Richard Owen called it *Labyrinthodon* in 1841.
  - Edward Drinker Cope coined Stegocephalia in 1868.
  - D. M. S. Watson revived Labyrinthodontia in 1919.
  - Gunnar Säve-Söderbergh (Swedish) removed embolomeres from the group, and Alfred Romer brought the name Temnospondyli back into use.
- **Sizes:**
  - *Prionosuchus* of Brazil, 9 m long, is the largest known amphibian.
  - Capitosauroids were 2.3–4 m long.
  - *Koolasuchus*, the last known temnospondyl (Early Cretaceous, Australia), weighed about 500 kg.
- **Key dates and events:**
  - The Triassic–Jurassic extinction (about 199.6 Mya) killed most groups; brachyopoids survived.

**Source 14: Dvorak technique**

- **What it is:** a system for estimating tropical cyclone intensity from visible and infrared satellite images only. Vernon Dvorak developed it between 1969 and 1984. He began in 1969 using images of northwest Pacific cyclones.
- **Patterns and T-ranges:**
  - Curved band: T1.0–4.5
  - Shear: T1.5–3.5
  - Central dense overcast (CDO): T2.5–5.0. T2.5 equals minimal tropical storm, 40 mph (65 km/h).
  - Banding eye: T4.0–4.5
  - Eye: T4.5–8.0. Uses the temperature difference between the warm eye and the cold cloud tops; a larger difference means a stronger storm.
  - Central cold cover (CCC): rare, and indicates little development.
- **T-number and CI:** the T-number (Tropical Number) and Current Intensity (CI) range from 1 to 8.
  - For weakening storms, the CI is held for 12 hours; NHC research suggests 6 hours is more reasonable.
  - Change is limited to 2.5 T-numbers per 24 hours.
- **Pressure corrections:** original pressure estimates were 5–10 hPa too low in the Atlantic and up to 20 hPa too high in the northwest Pacific.
  - Atkinson and Holliday devised a northwest Pacific wind-pressure relationship in 1975, modified in 1977.
- **Objective versions:** the objective technique began in 1998, the advanced version in 2004, and a latitude/tropopause pressure bias was found in 2004.
  - Automated methods use 6-hour averaging.
  - CIMSS at the University of Wisconsin–Madison developed the Objective Dvorak Technique (ODT).
- **Agencies issuing Dvorak numbers:** NHC's TAFB; NOAA/NESDIS SAB; JTWC in Pearl Harbor, Hawaii.
- **Example:** TD 24, later Hurricane Wilma (2005). TAFB and SAB gave T2.5/35 kt, but the intensity was set at only 30 kt because surface winds lag the satellite signature by about 12 hours.
- **Agency practices:**
  - CMA plans to adopt the 1984 version.
  - IMD prefers visible imagery.
  - JMA uses infrared.
  - Hong Kong Observatory and JMA continue using Dvorak after landfall.
  - Cyclone Center (citizen science) categorizes post-1970 storms.
- **Accuracy:** within 5 mph (8 km/h) of aircraft measurements half the time. Least certain between 60 and 100 mph. Refinements in 1972–77 changed intensities by up to 20 mph.
- **Limitations:** cannot assess subtropical cyclones (led to Hebert-Poteat technique, 1975). Underestimates extratropical transition (led to Miller and Lander technique).

**Source 25: Oxaziridine**

- **Definition:** A three-membered heterocycle of O, N and C. Its strained ring and weak N–O bond allow electrophilic transfer of both O and N. Nucleophiles attack N when the N-substituent is small (R1 = H) and attack O when it is bulky.

**Source 28: Constant k filter**

**Main claims**
- Constant k (k-type) filters are electronic filters designed with the **image method**. They are the original and simplest filters from that method, built as a **ladder network of identical passive sections**.
- They were the first filters that could approach an ideal frequency response to any prescribed limit by adding enough sections.
- They are rarely used in modern design because more accurate methodologies have superseded them. The ladder topology survives in modern designs such as the **Tchebyscheff filter**.

**People, organisations and dates**
- Invented by **George Campbell**, who published in **1922**. He had invented them earlier, since his colleague at **AT&T Co**, **Otto Zobel**, was already improving the design by then.
- Campbell called them "electric wave filters."
- Zobel invented the **m-derived filter** and coined the term "constant k" to distinguish Campbell's design from it.
- Purpose: separating **multiplexed telephone channels** on transmission lines. Later use became much wider.
- They were superior to the RL circuit and other single-element filters because any degree of stop-band rejection or transition steepness could be reached by adding sections.
- Campbell gave low-pass, high-pass and band-pass designs. Band-stop and multiple-band versions are also possible.

**Technical details**
- Series elements have impedance 2Z and shunt elements have admittance 2Y. The factor of 2 is for convenience and disappears in half-sections.
- The mid-series section has image impedance **ZiT** (T topology). The mid-shunt section has **ZiΠ** (Π topology).
- "L" means the section's shape (an inverted L), not inductance.
- k has units of **ohms**, and Y must be the dual of Z.
- k is the characteristic impedance Z0 of the limiting transmission line. It equals the image impedance at resonance (band-pass) or at ω = 0 (low-pass).
- With no resistive elements, image impedance is real in the pass band and imaginary in the stop band. Pass-band transmission is lossless, with phase change only.

**Source 35: Shackleton (crater)**
