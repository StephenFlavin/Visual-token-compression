**Source 1: Tropical Storm Josephine (2008)**

**Overview:** Tenth tropical storm of the 2008 Atlantic hurricane season. It weakened mainly because of wind shear and dry air, and its only notable effect was minor flooding on St. Croix.

**Timeline:**
- **Aug 31:** A strong tropical wave moved off the African coast.
- **Sept 2:** The wave became Tropical Depression Ten, 170 mi (270 km) south-southeast of the Cape Verde Islands. It was upgraded to Tropical Storm Josephine about six hours later, around noon. An eye-like feature formed in the upper levels.
- **Sept 3:** Peak intensity of 65 mph (100 km/h), reached around 8 a.m. EDT.
  - Weakening followed from shear (partly from Hurricane Ike's outflow and a southward-diving upper-level trough) and dry air, with arc clouds signalling the dry air.
  - The National Hurricane Center (NHC) was unsure of the storm's true intensity because it lacked accurate wind readings.
- **Sept 4–5:** Convection repeatedly redeveloped and was lost.
  - Winds fell to 40 mph (65 km/h).
  - Downgraded to a tropical depression at 11 p.m. EDT with 35 mph (55 km/h) winds. It was kept as a depression rather than a remnant low because regeneration seemed possible.
- **Sept 6:** Weakened to a tropical depression (per the summary), then became a remnant low.
- **Sept 7:** Signs of redevelopment.
- **Sept 8:** Shear exposed the low again.
- **Sept 9:** Degenerated into an open wave.
- **Sept 10:** The summary says the low dissipated 520 mi (835 km) east of Guadeloupe. The detailed section instead says the remnants redeveloped and headed toward the Bahamas.
- **Sept 14:** Remnants dissipated entirely.

**Impacts:**
- **Cape Verde:** About 0.55 in (14 mm) of rain fell, with no damage or flooding.
- **St. Croix:** Several days later, up to 1 in (25.4 mm) of rain caused minor street and urban flooding, with no known damage.

**Source 2: Mount Elbert**

- **Status:** Highest summit of the Rocky Mountains, of Colorado, and of the Mississippi River drainage basin. At 14,440 ft (4,401.2 m) it is the highest peak in the Sawatch Range and the 2nd-highest in the contiguous US after Mount Whitney, which is also its parent peak. It ranks 14th-highest in the US including Alaska and Hawaii.
- **Location:** San Isabel National Forest, Lake County. It lies 12.1 mi (19.4 km) SW of Leadville, bearing 223°. Leadville is also described as ~16 mi (26 km) NE and is the nearest large town. Twin Lakes lies at its base. Denver is ~130 mi (209 km) E, Vail 50 mi (80 km) N, and Aspen 40 mi (64 km) W.
- **Neighbours:** Mount Massive (N) is 2nd-highest in the Rockies and 3rd in the contiguous US, only 12 ft (3.7 m) lower. La Plata Peak (S) is 5th in the Rockies.
- **Name:** Honours Samuel Hitt Elbert, Territorial Governor 1873–74. Miners named it after his September 1873 Ute treaty opened over 3,000,000 acres (12,000 km²) to mining and rail.
- **History:**
  - First recorded ascent: Henry W. Stuckle, Hayden Survey, 1874.
  - Height was 14,433 ft (4,399 m) under the 1929 datum and changed in 1988 (NAVD 1988), which sparked protests.
  - After the Great Depression, Massive supporters built stone piles on its summit to raise it; Elbert backers demolished them.
  - First motorized ascent was by Jeep in 1949.
  - *Science* (July 1894) reported a summit electrical storm.
  - Orator Anna Elizabeth Dickinson rode up on a US government mule.
- **Geology:**
  - Part of the Laramide uplift; the Sawatch separated from the Mosquito Range ~28 million years ago.
  - Mostly quartzite; the summit ridge is Precambrian basement ~1.7 billion years old.
  - Turquoise Lake is formed by the Sugar Loaf Dam; Twin Lakes by end moraines.
- **Routes:** All gain over 4,100 ft.
  - North Elbert: 4.6 mi, ~4,500 ft gain.
  - South Elbert: 5.5 mi, 4,600 ft gain.
  - Black Cloud: Class 2, 10–14 hr, 5,300 ft gain, crosses South Elbert (14,134 ft).
  - Climbers are advised to start by 6 a.m.

**Source 4: Japanese battleship Asahi**

- **Type and builder:** Pre-dreadnought battleship for the IJN. Ordered in the 1897 programme from Clydebank Engineering & Shipbuilding Co., Clydebank, Scotland. John Brown & Company bought the firm and completed her.
- **Funding:** Part of Japan's 1896 ten-year build-up of 6 battleships and 6 armoured cruisers, paid from China's £30,000,000 indemnity.
- **Key dates:** Laid down 1 Aug 1898; launched 13 Mar 1899; completed 31 Jul 1900. Completion was delayed about 3 months after she ran aground off Southsea. Arrived Yokosuka 23 Oct 1900. Flagship of the Standing Fleet from 22 May 1901.
- **Design:** Modified Formidable class. Length 425 ft 3 in (129.6 m); displacement 15,200 long tons; crew about 773. Engines by Humphrys, Tennant with 25 Belleville boilers, rated 15,000 ihp. Design speed 18 kn; reached 18.3 kn on trials, 23 Mar 1900. Range 9,000 nmi at 10 kn.
- **Armament:** Four 12-in Elswick guns, fourteen 6-in guns, twenty 12-pounders, four 18-in torpedo tubes. Harvey armour belt up to 9 in.
- **Russo-Japanese War:**
  - Port Arthur, 9 Feb 1904 (Capt. Hikohachi Yamada): not hit.
  - Yellow Sea, 10 Aug 1904 (Capt. Tsunaakira Nomoto): her shells killed Vice Adm. Wilgelm Vitgeft on Tsesarevich.
  - Struck a mine 26 Oct 1904; repaired at Sasebo, Nov 1904 to Apr 1905.
  - Tsushima, 27 May 1905: fired 142 twelve-inch shells, the most of any ship; hit 6 times. Capt. W. C. Pakenham (Royal Navy) observed the battle from her quarterdeck.
- **Later service:**
  - Siberian Intervention: guard ship at Kamchatka, Jan to Aug 1918.
  - Coastal defence ship from 1 Sep 1921; disarmed by Jul 1923 under the Washington Naval Treaty.
  - Converted to submarine salvage ship 1925–27. Catapult launch of an E2N1 seaplane, May 1928. Placed in reserve 1928.
  - Converted to repair ship, completed 18 Dec 1938; based at Shanghai, then Cam Ranh Bay.
  - Repaired light cruiser Naka at Singapore, Apr 1942.
- **Loss:** Sunk by USS Salmon on 25/26 May 1942, about 100 mi SE of Cape Padaran, at 10°N 110°E. 16 killed; captain and 582 crew rescued by CH-9.

**Source 6: J. C. W. Beckham**

**Life:** John Crepps Wickliffe Beckham (Aug 5, 1869 – Jan 9, 1940), 35th Governor of Kentucky and US Senator. He was Kentucky's first popularly elected senator after the 17th Amendment. Born at Wickland, near Bardstown, Nelson County. Parents: William Netherton and Julia Tevis (Wickliffe) Beckham. Grandfather Charles A. Wickliffe was KY governor (1839–40) and Postmaster General under John Tyler. Uncle Robert C. Wickliffe was governor of Louisiana.

**Early career:** House page in 1881, aged 12. Attended Roseland Academy and Central University, leaving at 17. Bardstown schools principal 1888–93. Law degree from the University of Kentucky, 1889. KY House from 1894 (four terms); Speaker in 1898.

**1899–1900:** Running mate of William Goebel, though under 30. Goebel lost to Republican William S. Taylor, was shot Jan 30, 1900, and was declared winner the next day. Courts upheld Beckham (Court of Appeals Apr 6; US Supreme Court declined May 21, 1900; John Marshall Harlan dissented). Taylor fled to Indianapolis. Beckham won the Nov 6, 1900 special election over John W. Yerkes by fewer than 4,000 votes. Married Jean Raphael Fuqua; two sons.

**Governorship:** Beat Morris B. Belknap in 1903. Signed the Day Law (Mar 1904); Berea College lost 8–1 in 1908. Beckham County was dissolved Apr 29, 1904. Term ended Dec 10, 1907.

**Senate:** Lost 1908 to William O. Bradley on the 29th ballot; four Democrats defected over prohibition. Won 1914 over Augustus E. Willson by 32,000 votes. Lost 1920 to Richard P. Ernst (50.3% vs 49.7%).

**Later:** Lost the 1927 governor race to Flem D. Sampson by more than 32,000 votes; the Jockey Club spent over $500,000. Lost the 1936 Senate race; M. M. Logan won by 2,385 votes. Buried in Frankfort Cemetery. Beckham County, Oklahoma, is named for him. Wickland was added to the National Register of Historic Places Feb 16, 1973.

**Source 7: 1888 - 89 New Zealand Native football team**

  - Lost to Middlesex 9–0 at Sheffield Park.

  - Beat Yorkshire 10–6, then lost the return match 16–4 on 19 Jan 1889, their largest defeat.
  - Lost to Wales 5–0 at Swansea, 22 Dec 1888. Norman Biggs debuted aged 18 years 49 days. Wales adopted the four three-quarter system after this match.
  - Biggest win: Somerset 17–4.

  - Frank Evershed scored a disputed try while Stoddart's shorts were being replaced.

- Overall rugby record: 107 matches, 78 wins, 6 draws, 23 losses.
- Legacy: contributed to the formation of the NZRFU in 1892. Ellison and Gage later captained NZ. Inducted into the IRB Hall of Fame in 2008.

**Source 9: Battle of Sullivan's Island**

- **Date/place:** June 28, 1776, near Charleston, South Carolina. Also called the Battle of Fort Sullivan or the First Siege of Charleston. The British returned in 1780 and took the city.
- **British commanders:** General Henry Clinton and Admiral Sir Peter Parker. Major General Charles Cornwallis brought 2,500 troops from Ireland, departing February 13, 1776, escorted by 11 warships.
- **Approach:** Clinton left Boston January 20 and reached Cape Fear on March 12. The Loyalists had already been defeated at Moore's Creek Bridge. The fleet left Cape Fear May 31 and reached Charleston June 1.
- **American commanders:** Colonel William Moultrie, aged 46, held the fort with 435 men. General Charles Lee commanded the southern theater; he called the fort a "slaughter pen" and planned to replace Moultrie on June 27. John Rutledge was president of the General Assembly. Other forces: about 2,000 local troops, 1,900 Continentals and 2,700 militia.
- **Fort:** Built of palmetto logs and sand, with walls 20 ft high and 16 ft wide, and 31 cannon. The spongy palmetto absorbed the shot.
- **British fleet:** Flagship Bristol (50 guns), Experiment (50 guns), Active, Solebay, Sphinx, Syren, Actaeon, Friendship and the bomb vessel Thunder, nearly 300 cannon in total.
- **Land plan:** 2,200 troops landed on Long Island (now the Isle of Palms). On June 17 the channel (Breach Inlet) proved shoulder-deep. Colonel William Thomson's men repelled Clinton's boats.
- **Battle:** Fighting ran from about 9 am to 9 pm. Three frigates ran aground; Actaeon was burned the next day. Parker was wounded.
- **Casualties:** Bristol 40 killed and 71 wounded, hit more than 70 times. Experiment 23 killed and 56 wounded. Americans 12 killed and 25 wounded.
- **Aftermath:** The fleet left on July 21 for New York. The fort was renamed Fort Moultrie and passed to the National Park Service in 1960. Sergeant William Jasper re-raised the Moultrie ("Liberty") flag, which was blue with a white crescent.

**Source 10: Hu Zhengyan**

- **Who:** Hu Zhengyan (胡正言), c. 1584 to 1674. Chinese artist, printmaker, seal-carver and calligrapher, but primarily a publisher. Born in Xiuning County, Anhui, in 1584 or early 1585. Died in poverty aged 90, in late 1673 or early 1674.
- **Family:** His father and elder brother Zhengxin (art name Wusuo) were physicians. After age 30, Hu travelled with them around Lu'an and Huoshan. Claims that Hu himself was a doctor appear only in late-19th-century sources. His wife was Wu.
- **Studio:** By 1619 he lived on Jilongshan in Nanjing. He named his home the Ten Bamboo Studio (Shizhuzhai) after ten bamboo plants growing in front of it. He employed ten artisans, including his brothers Zhengxin and Zhengxing and his sons Qipu and Qiyi.
- **Ming loyalty:** He carved a seal in 1644 for the Hongguang Emperor (Zhu Yousong), presented with the essay *Great Exhortation of the Seal*. He declined the post of Drafter for the Secretariat (zhongshu sheren). The emperor was captured and died in 1645, and Hu went into seclusion in 1646. He renamed the studio Digutang ("Hall Rooted in the Past").
- **Disputed history:** Wen Ruilin's *Lost History of the South* (published 1830) says Hu was promoted to the Hanlin Academy. This account is possibly fabricated.
- **Visitors:** Xiao Yuncong visited him in 1667 and Lü Liuliang in 1673.
- **Seal-carving:** He followed the Huizhou school founded by He Zhen. Zhou Lianggong praised his work.
- **Publishing:** The studio published over 20 books between 1627 and 1644 and no fiction. Its first book was *Tested Prescriptions for Myriad Illnesses* (1631, reissued ten years later). Other titles include *Four Books* (1640), *Shi Tan* (1635), and *Thousand Character Classic* (1663, with Li Deng).
- **Printing techniques:** Douban "assorted block" printing used 30 to 50 blocks and up to 70 inkings per image. Gonghua embossing was invented by Wu Faxiang (1626).
- **Manual (1633):** About 320 prints by about 30 artists, in 8 sections, with butterfly binding. It stayed in print for about 200 years. Cambridge digitised it in August 2015 (quote from Charles Aylmer). It influenced the Mustard Seed Garden manual and Japanese nishiki-e.
- **Letter Paper (1644):** Reissued 1934 to 1941 by Zheng Zhenduo and Lu Xun, and again in 1952.

**Source 11: Raid on Manila (1798)**

- **What it was:** A Royal Navy false-flag raid in January 1798, during the French Revolutionary Wars. Its aims were to scout Manila's defences, capture a Manila galleon and assess the Spanish squadron there. The motive was protecting the China Fleet (East Indiamen sailing Macau→Britain). One 1804 convoy was worth over £8 million (≈£600 million in 2016).
- **Background:**
  - Spain switched sides after the Treaty of San Ildefonso (1796).
  - Rear-Admiral Peter Rainier, with Governor-General Sir John Shore and Col. Arthur Wellesley, planned an invasion in 1797. It was cancelled after news of the Treaty of Campo Formio (August 1797) and the threat from Tipu Sultan of Mysore.
  - Sercey's six frigates had been bluffed off in the Bali Strait Incident (January 1797).
- **Convoy and departure:** Capt. Edward Cooke in HMS Sybille (40 guns) escorted a convoy from Bombay, which reached Macau on 13 Dec 1797. HMS Fox (32 guns) was under Capt. Pulteney Malcolm. Cooke sailed on 5 Jan 1798 with linguist Mr. Bernard aboard Fox. He took 3,900 silver dollars from a Spanish merchant vessel.
- **At Manila:**
  - The ships arrived on 13 Jan 1798, disguised as the French frigates Forte and Prudente; Cooke posed as Commodore Latour.
  - The Spanish ships San Pedro, Europa, Montañés, Maria de la Cabeya and Luisa were under repair at Cavite (typhoon damage, April 1797).
  - The galleon Marquesetta had already been unloaded, a precaution taken because HMS Resistance (Capt. Edward Pakenham) was in the area.
  - The barge of Rear-Adm. Ignacio María de Álava was among the boats lured in. About 200 Spanish were taken prisoner and later released.
  - Three gunboats were seized at the Pasig River (one 32-pounder, two 24-pounders). One was lost on 19 Jan with 12 crew.
- **Zamboanga:** Attacked on 22 Jan (governor Raymundo Español) and repulsed. 250 lance-armed villagers drove off the landing. Spanish losses were 1 killed and 4 wounded; about 450 cannonballs were later recovered.
- **"Pullock," 27 Jan:** Lumad tribesmen killed 2 and captured 9; Sultan Kibab Sahriyal was involved in their recovery. Total British losses for the mission were 18 lives.
- **Assessments:** C. Northcote Parkinson and Richard Woodman ("no glorious outcome").
- **Aftermath:** Capt. William Hargood fought the Franco-Spanish force off Macau in February 1799. Cooke captured Forte on 28 Feb 1799 near Balasore but died of his wounds on 25 May.

**Source 12: B of the Bang**

- **What:** Sculpture by Thomas Heatherwick, designed to look like an exploding firework. Commissioned by New East Manchester Limited to mark the 2002 Commonwealth Games. Design chosen in a 2002 competition by a panel of residents and art experts; approved early 2003.
- **Where:** Next to the City of Manchester Stadium, Sportcity, Beswick, at the corner of Alan Turing Way and Ashton New Road.
- **Name:** From Linford Christie's remark that he started races at "the B of the Bang". Nicknamed "KerPlunk" and "G of the Bang" (because of delays).
- **Rank:** UK's tallest sculpture until Aspire (University of Nottingham) in 2008. It was taller and leaned more than the Leaning Tower of Pisa, and was over twice the height of the Angel of the North (20 m/66 ft).
- **Specs:**
  - 56 m (184 ft) tall, angled at 30°.
  - 180 hollow steel spikes, each 2.1 m long.
  - Five 25 m legs joining the spikes 22 m up.
  - Weighed 165 tonnes; foundations of over 1,000 tonnes of concrete, including a 400 m² slab, 20 m deep.
  - Made of Cor-Ten weathering steel; withstood winds over 100 mph.
  - A time capsule (to be opened c. 2300) is now lost.
- **Build:** Made in Sheffield by Heatherwick Studio, Packman Lucas, Flint and Neill and Westbury Structures. Core arrived 13 June 2004 under police escort and was lifted in August 2004. Unveiled by Christie on 12 January 2005.
- **Cost:** £1.42m, twice the estimate. Funded by the ERDF (£700,000), the North West Development Agency (£500,000) and Manchester City Council (£120,000).
- **Failures:**
  - 6 January 2005: a spike tip fell.
  - May 2005: firefighters cut off a loose spike.
  - May 2006: nine spikes removed for analysis.
- **Legal action and removal:** Legal action announced October 2007; £1.7m out-of-court settlement in November 2008. Dismantling recommended January 2009, announced February 2009; Antony Gormley objected. Removal by Connell Brothers began April 2009. The core was sold for scrap for £17,000 in July 2012; the spikes remain in storage.

**Source 30: Nazarov cyclization reaction**

**Main claims:**
- The Nazarov cyclization is an organic reaction that makes cyclopentenones. It has classical and modern variants.
- It was discovered by Ivan Nikolaevich Nazarov (1906–1957) in 1941, while he was studying rearrangements of allyl vinyl ketones.
- Classical form: a divinyl ketone is activated by a stoichiometric Lewis or protic acid. The key step is a cationic 4π conrotatory electrocyclization (Woodward-Hoffmann rules): pentadienyl cation → oxyallyl cation → β-H elimination → tautomerization.
- Shoppe first showed experimentally that the mechanism is intramolecular.
- The reaction requires the s-trans/s-trans conformation. It is a rare example of a stereoselective, rather than stereospecific, pericyclic reaction.
- Cyclopentenones occur in natural products such as jasmone, the aflatoxins and some prostaglandins.
- Research was quiet until the mid-1980s, with syntheses of Trichodiene and Nor-Sterepolide.
- Drawbacks of the classical form: strong acids (TiCl4, BF3, MeSO3H), stoichiometric promoter, poor regioselectivity of elimination, loss of a stereocenter, and epimer mixtures.

**Researchers:**
- **Scott Denmark** (University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign), mid-1980s: silicon-directed (β-silicon effect) variant using FeCl3; applied to the synthesis of Silphinene.
- **Alison Frontier** (University of Rochester): "polarized" Nazarov cyclizations catalysed by copper triflate.
- **Marcus Tius** (University of Hawaii): allenyl vinyl ketones; a chiral diosphenol in 64% yield and 95% ee; a camphor auxiliary used in the first asymmetric synthesis of roseophilin (solvent HFIP/trifluoroethanol).
- **Fredrick G. West** (University of Alberta): oxyallyl cation trapping ("interrupted" Nazarov).
- **Varinder Aggarwal**: first chiral Lewis acid asymmetric version, using Cu(II) bisoxazoline, up to 98% ee.

**Other details:**
- A pentadienyl cation can also come from silver-catalysed ring opening of dichlorocyclopropanes, or from an epoxide in the rocaglamide synthesis.
- An iridium-catalysed tandem Nazarov/Michael reaction uses β-nitrostyrene.
- Named variants: iso-, homo-, vinylogous, retro- and imino-Nazarov.
